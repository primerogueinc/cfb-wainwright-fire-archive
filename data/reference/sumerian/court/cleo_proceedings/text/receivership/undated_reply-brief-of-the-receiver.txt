I.      INTRODUCTION

1.      This Reply Brief of Law is submitted by Alvarez & Marsal Canada Inc. (“A&M”), in its
        capacity as Court-appointed receiver and manager (the “Receiver”) of the undertakings,
        properties and assets of 2755857 Alberta Ltd. (“ResidualCo”) is filed in response to the
        Application of Canadian Natural Resources Limited (“CNRL”) filed September 23, 2026
        (the “Application”) and CNRL’s Bench Brief of Law filed September 23, 2026 (the “CNRL
        Brief”).

2.      CNRL asks the Court to excuse its failure to comply with a Court-ordered Claims
        Procedure (defined herein) and permit a claim filed more than five months after the Claims
        Bar Date (defined herein).

3.      This is not a case of an unsophisticated creditor who lacked notice or inadvertently missed
        a deadline. CNRL is one of Canada's largest and most sophisticated commercial entities.
        At all times throughout the receivership proceedings it was represented by counsel and
        actively participated in matters relating to cure costs. CNRL ultimately negotiated directly
        with 269 Corp (defined herein) regarding those cure costs and pursued a consent
        judgment against 269 Corp. for payment of those cure costs. In doing so, CNRL elected
        not to monitor the receivership proceedings and it did not take any steps to preserve a
        contingent claim or make a placeholder claim through the Claims Procedure.

4.      CNRL's failure to make a contingent or placeholder claim was not the product of mistake
        or inadvertence. Rather, it was the result of a deliberate commercial decision to pursue
        recovery from 269 Corp. instead of preserving its rights through the Court-approved
        Claims Procedure.

5.      Having pursued that strategy and to date having failed to recover from 269 Corp., which
        is an operating, going-concern entity, CNRL now seeks to shift the consequences of its
        decision onto the receivership estate and the stakeholders who complied with the Claims
        Procedure Order. The law does not support such relief.

6.      The equitable considerations articulated in the jurisprudence overwhelmingly favour
        finality, certainty, and the enforcement of Court-ordered claims processes.

7.      The Receiver has, in accordance with the Claims Procedure Order, provided a Notice of
        Disallowance regarding the CNRL claim, as it was submitted past the Claims Bar Date
        and the claim of CNRL is with 269 Corp., not ResidualCo or its estate.

         in the Claims Procedure notwithstanding it was filed after the Claims Bar Date.

IV.      LAW AND ARGUMENT

A.       The CNRL Claim Should Not Be Allowed

21.      The Court possesses discretion to permit a late-filed claim. However, that discretion is
         equitable in nature and must be exercised judicially having regard to the circumstances of
         the case.

22.     In Blue Range, the Court of Appeal of Alberta identified the relevant considerations for
        admitting a late filed claim, including:

        (a)     was the delay caused by inadvertence and if so, did the claimant act in good faith;

        (b)     what is the effect of permitting the claim in terms of the existence and impact of
                any relevant prejudice caused by the delay;

        (c)     if relevant prejudice is found, can it be alleviated by attaching appropriate
                conditions to an order permitting late filing; and

        (d)     if relevant prejudice is found which cannot be alleviated, are there any other
                considerations which may nonetheless warrant an order permitting late filing. 12

23.     The Court’s discretion must also be informed by the fundamental insolvency principles of
        certainty, finality, and the orderly administration of estates. Court-approved claims
        procedures are not merely administrative conveniences. They are essential tools that
        permit receivers, creditors, regulators, and other stakeholders to ascertain liabilities,
        negotiate resolutions, and distribute estate assets with confidence and transparency to all
        creditors and stakeholders.

24.     If sophisticated creditors are permitted to disregard claims bar dates and seek relief only
        after alternative recovery efforts prove unsuccessful, the integrity of claims procedures in
        insolvency proceedings would be materially undermined.

Inadvertence and Good Faith

25.     When reviewing the first Blue Range criterion, “inadvertent” includes carelessness,
        negligence, accident, and is unintentional. It does not support a creditor “lying in the
        weeds” and seeking to secure some advantage by late filing. 13

26.     CNRL characterizes its failure to file the CNRL Claim as inadvertent, however the
        evidence demonstrates otherwise.

27.     CNRL’s failure to file its CNRL Claim before the Claims Bar Date was a deliberate strategic
        decision rather than inadvertence. As recognized in SemCanada, a creditor who
        knowingly elects to pursue an alternative recovery strategy cannot later characterize that

         participating in the Claims Procedure. Further, it made a bargain with 269 Corp. that
         satisfied the Pre-Filing Balance for the purposes of the Claims Procedure. That strategy

         of distributions for cure costs recoveries at the time of entering into the Settlement
         Agreement. The fact that the strategy to date has been unsuccessful does not transform
         that choice into inadvertence.

31.      The equitable relief recognized in Blue Range is intended to address genuine mistakes,

44.     In respect of materiality, if accepted, the CNRL Claim would reduce the payments to the

45.     When assessing prejudice, Court look to the question of whether a creditor by reason of
        the late filings lose a realistic opportunity to do anything that they otherwise might have
        done. 29 The settlement was reached between the AER and Municipalities without the

        no similar understanding with regard to the CNRL Claim.

46.     The prejudice factor weighs against the Court overturning the Notice of Disallowance and
        accepting the late filed CNRL Claim.

Other Considerations

50.     It is neither fair nor equitable to set aside the Notice of Disallowance and approve the late
        filed claim of CNRL.

51.     To allow a creditor with full knowledge of the Claims Procedure to ignore the Claims Bar
        Date and file a significant, late claim, disrupts a negotiated settlement and may
        unnecessarily extend these Receivership Proceedings.

52.     CNRL has no good or satisfactory reason to offer as to why it failed to file a contingent
        claim except that it was no longer monitoring the Receivership Proceedings.

53.     The policy reasons that emphasize the need for certainty and finality in an approved claims
        procedure and fair treatment to all creditors outweigh the prejudice to CNRL of disallowing
        a late claim.

The Settlement Agreement and Consent Order released ResidualCo

54.     CNRL argues that neither the Settlement Agreement nor the Consent Order expressly
        released ResidualCo. That submission misses the point.

55.     The Receiver does not contend that the Settlement Agreement contains an express
        release of ResidualCo. Rather, the significance of the Settlement Agreement and the
        Consent Order is that they demonstrate CNRL’s election to pursue the collection of the
        Pre-Filing Balance from 269 Corp instead of participating in the Claims Procedure.

56.     CNRL contends that “cure costs” must be paid current by the purchaser of contractual
        assignment. 31 CNRL negotiated a direct contractual obligation from 269 Corp. to satisfy
        the Pre-Filing Balance, which aligns with the requirement of a purchaser to pay cure costs.
        CNRL negotiated this with 269 Corp. It is unclear to the Receiver how this commercially
        transferred liability would jointly remain with ResidualCo.

57.     In this case the payment of cure costs to facilitate the assignment of a contract was an
        obligation taken on by 269 Corp. The liability did not transfer to ResidualCo. If there is an
        agreed assignment of the obligation, then the claim of CNRL is with 269 Corp.

58.     The converse would place CNRL in a materially better position than other creditors. Under
        CNRL’s approach, it would retain the benefit of a direct contractual payment obligation
        from 269 Corp. and still preserve a full claim against the ResidualCo estate for the same
        indebtedness, as if the estate is a guarantor of the arrangement between CNRL and 269
        Corp. That would afford CNRL multiple avenues of recovery unavailable to other creditors
        and effectively allow CNRL to improve its position through private arrangements
        negotiated without the Receiver being party.

59.     CNRL seeks to preserve multiple recovery sources for the same debt, while other creditors
        are required to look solely to the Claims Procedure and the ResidualCo estate. Permitting
        this would undermine the fairness, finality, and certainty that the Claims Procedure Order.

60.     The Settlement Agreement and Consent Order may not contain an express release of
        ResidualCo for the CNRL Claim, however, by the nature of the agreements they act as a
        release of ResidualCo. The CNRL Claim is not with ResidualCo but is with 269 Corp.

V.      CONCLUSION

61.     The Receiver requests that this Honourable Court dismiss the Application to overturn the
        Notice of Disallowance and confirm the late claim filed by CNRL is barred in accordance
        with the Claims Bar Date established by the Claims Procedure Order.
