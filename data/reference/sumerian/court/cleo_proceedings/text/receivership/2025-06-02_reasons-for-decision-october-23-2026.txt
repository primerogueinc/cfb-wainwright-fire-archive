I.      Introduction and Background

[1]    On June 2, 2025, the Court granted a receivership order (Receivership Order) appointing
a receiver-manager (Receiver) 1 in respect of Cleo Energy Corp. (Cleo) 2. Cleo’s employees
(Former Employees) were terminated shortly thereafter. The Minister under the Wage Earner
Protection Program Act, SC 2005, c 47, s 1 (WEPPA) accepted the Former Employees’ entitlement
to and made payments (WEPP Payments) under the Wage Earner Protection Program (WEPP).

[2]     In October 2025, the Court dismissed the Receiver’s application for a reverse vesting order
(RVO) approving the sale of Cleo’s shares to a purchaser (269 Corp 3) under a share purchase
agreement (Share Transaction) due to insufficient evidence of the need for an RVO transaction,
without prejudice to its ability to reapply on better evidence: Cleo Energy Corp (Re), 2025 ABKB
621 [Decision]. In the Decision, the Court also raised concerns about two issues that had been
raised in the Receiver’s RVO application: (1) RVO transactions that do not provide for payment
of “cure costs” for retained contracts; and (2) the idea that the Minister might take steps to claw
back the WEPP Payments following approval of an RVO transaction.

[3]     On November 14, 2025, upon receiving further evidence, the Court approved the Share
Transaction under a revised RVO (Revised RVO) that addressed the Court’s cure costs concern.
The Revised RVO provided for priority payment for retained contract cure costs out of the
purchase price proceeds, failing which the contracting counter-party could waive their entitlement
to the cure costs and terminate the retained contract.

               2.      Legal Framework

[34] The request to make the WEPPA Declarations engages the limits on the Court’s
discretionary jurisdiction to provide advice and directions and grant declarations in receiverships.

[35] The court has the jurisdiction to provide advice and directions in receiverships, under both
the BIA and the court’s broad general jurisdiction under the Judicature Act, RSA 2000, c J-2: BIA,
section 249; RIC New Brunswick Inc v Telecommunications Research Laboratories, 2010
ABCA 227 at para 23; Judicature Act, sections 8, 13(2).

[36] It has been held that advice and directions are appropriate where the relief “relates to the
receiver’s administration, the powers and performance of duties including custody of assets, their
disposition and approval of settlements”: Griffiths McBurney & Partners v Ernst & Young YBM
Inc, 2000 ABCA 284 [YBM Magnex] at para 30, citing Bennett on Receiverships, 2nd ed
(Toronto: Carswell, 1999) at 166.

[37] However, there are limits on the court’s exercise of its advice and direction jurisdiction.
Courts generally should decline to provide advice and directions in a receivership where:

       (a)     the requested advice and directions do not directly relate to the receivership;

       (b)     the advice and directions seek answers to theoretical questions;

       (c)     the receiver is effectively using the court as a legal adviser;

       (d)     the court is asked to decide disputed substantive matters and an affected party has
               not had the opportunity to fairly participate, or a summary determination is not
               appropriate;

       (e)     it would substantively impact proceedings in another jurisdiction which are
               properly before the court in that other jurisdiction; or

       (f)     where it would preclude a substantive defence in other existing litigation
               proceedings.

       See, for example: YBM Magnex at paras 34, 46, 48; Canada (Attorney General) v Ernst
       & Young Inc, 2019 ABCA 180 at para 26; Calgary Firefighters’ Supplementary Pension
       Plan (Trustee of) v Calgary (City of), 2004 ABQB 125 at para 80.
                                                    9


[38] The court’s jurisdiction to grant declaratory relief under section 11 of the Judicature Act,
is a statutory and equitable remedy: Wesley v Alberta, 2024 ABCA 276 at para 77, leave to appeal
refused 2025 CanLII 44340 (SCC); YBM Magnex at para 56; Venini v Venini, 2023 ABKB 524
at para 63; Scenic Acres Community Association v Calgary (City), 2015 ABQB 586 at para 71;
Piikani Investment Corporation v Piikani First Nation, 2008 ABQB 775 at para 221; Romspen
Mortgage Ltd Partnership v 3443 Zen Garden Ltd Partnership, 2023 ABKB 730 at para 168,
aff’d 2024 ABCA 333.

[39] Declarations can be granted where: (1) the court has jurisdiction to hear the issue; (2) the
dispute is real and not theoretical; (3) the party raising the issue has a genuine interest in its
resolution; and (4) the respondent has an interest in opposing the declaration sought: Ewert v
Canada, 2018 SCC 30 at para 81; SA v Metro Vancouver Housing Corp, 2019 SCC 4 at para 60;
Daniels v Canada (Indian Affairs and Northern Development), 2016 SCC 12 at para 11; Canada
(Prime Minister) v Khadr, 2010 SCC 3 at para 46; Faichney v Fort McKay Métis Nation
Association, 2026 ABKB 429 at para 29.

[40] However, the discretion to grant declaratory relief should be exercised with care and
caution: YBM Magnex at para 55; Peter Lehmann Wines Ltd v Vintage West Wine Marketing
Inc, 2015 ABQB 481 at para 75; Scenic Acres at para 71. Whether to exercise the discretion is
based on a fact and context driven assessment.

[41]   Courts have held that discretionary declarations will not normally be appropriate where:

       (a)     it would interfere with the jurisdiction assigned to a specialized tribunal, or its
               purpose is to be used to influence or determine an issue extant before another court:
               Lobsinger v Lobsinger Family Trust, 2026 ABKB 671 at paras 19-21; Bosa v
               Canada, 2025 BCSC 1284 at para 50; Sheila Holmes Spousal Trust v Canada
               (Attorney General), 2013 ABQB 489 at paras 60-61; 2585929 Alberta Ltd (Re),
               2026 ABKB 75 at paras 26-29, 54-59; GLP NT Corp v Canada (Attorney
               General), 2003 CanLII 41554 (ON SC) at para 21; Baxter v Attorney General of
               Canada et al, 2013 ONSC 3153 at paras 23-26; 422252 Alberta Ltd v Canada
               (Attorney General), 2003 BCSC 1362 at paras 32, 42; Foundation v Jabs
               Construction Ltd, 1998 CanLII 4651 (BC SC) at para 7; Lau v Canada (Attorney
               General), 2014 BCSC 2384 at para 66;

       (b)     it would serve no purpose, which has been described as including where it would
               have no “practical utility”, would be “useless or embarrassing”, would not settle a
               live controversy between the parties, would be of no practical use to the applicant,
               would not lead to “any tangible result for the parties”, would be academic or moot,
               or has been “overtaken by subsequent events”: Iris Technologies Inc v Canada,
               2024 SCC 24 at para 58; YBM Magnex at paras 55-56, citing Hugh W Simmons
               Ltd v Foster, 1955 CanLII 49 (SCC), [1955] SCR 324 and 19 Hals. (2d) 215 at para
               512; Romspen Mortgage at para 168; Daniels at para 11; Faichney at para 31;
               Anderson v Canada (Employment, Workforce and Labour), 2019 ABQB 579 at
               para 13; CM v Alberta, 2024 ABCA 136 at paras 26, 29, citing Trang v Alberta
               (Edmonton Remand Centre), 2007 ABCA 263 at para 15, leave to appeal to SCC
               refused 2008 CanLII 6439 (SCC); Thomas v Rio Tinto Alcan Inc, 2024 BCCA 62
                                                   10


               at para 382, leave to appeal refused 2024 CanLII 96608 (SCC); Nassichuk-Dean v
               University of Lethbridge, 2022 ABKB 629 at paras 19-21;

       (c)     there exists an adequate alternative statutory mechanism, or more appropriate
               procedures, to resolve the dispute or protect the rights in question: Ewert at para
               83; YBM Magnex at para 55; Romspen Mortgage at para 169; Anderson at para
               14; Nova Scotia (Securities Commission) v Potter, 2012 NSCA 12 at para 19;
               Bryton Capital Corp GP Ltd v CIM Bayview Creek Inc, 2023 ONCA 363 at para
               64, citing Gook Country Estates Ltd v Quesnel (City of), 2008 BCCA 407 at para
               10; and

       (d)     it would be unjust based on equitable considerations, including the conduct of the
               party seeking the declaration and any equitable defences: YBM Magnex at para 55;
               Venini at para 63; Piikani Investment Corporation at para 221, citing Hong Kong
               Bank of Canada v Wheeler Holdings Ltd, 1993 CanLII 148 (SCC), [1993] 1 SCR
               167 at 192; Wawanesa Mutual Insurance Co v Lindblom, 2001 ABCA 102 at para
               53.

[42]   I now turn to this case.

               3.     This Court Has Jurisdiction but Declines to Exercise It

[43]   I find that this Court has jurisdiction to consider and grant the WEPPA Declarations.

[44] In addition to the broad statutory jurisdiction noted above, the Receivership Order
expressly provides that the Receiver may apply to the Court “for advice and directions in the
discharge of its powers and duties hereunder”. The Revised RVO also provides that the Court
“shall retain exclusive jurisdiction to, among other things, interpret, implement and enforce the
terms and provisions” of the Revised RVO, the Share Transaction, and disputes arising from them.

[45] Further, the WEPPA Declarations sought are connected to the administration of the
receivership. The Former Employees were terminated by the Receiver following the Receivership
Order, the Receiver paid the Superpriority Amount to ESDC for the WEPP Payments to the Former
Employees, and the Final Statement, Final Forecast and Proposed Distribution for which the
Receiver seeks approval are each calculated based on the Superpriority Amount having been paid.

[46] Finally, there is not clear, unequivocal or precise wording to oust the court’s jurisdiction:
Canada (Attorney General) v Fontaine, 2017 SCC 47 at para 33; Brewers Retail Inc v Campbell,
2023 ONCA 534 at para 64, citing Weber v Ontario Hydro, 1995 CanLII 108 (SCC), [1995] 2
SCR 929 at paras 50-58; Canada (Attorney General) v TeleZone Inc, 2010 SCC 62 at para 6;
1001270243 Ontario Inc, 2026 ONSC 3967 [Synaptive] at para 59.

[47] However, in the unique circumstances of this case there are several factors that lead me to
the conclusion that I should decline to exercise my discretion to consider or make the WEPPA
Declarations.

[48] First, the Receiver reasonably points to decisions in which courts have interpreted WEPPA
on the question of whether post-employment termination events are relevant to determining
                                                   11


employee eligibility for WEPP payments. The Receiver asserts that existing cases make it clear
that, as a matter of statutory interpretation of WEPPA and fairness, an RVO does not give the
Minister grounds to rescind WEPP payment entitlement because employee entitlement must be
considered at the time of termination and not based on later events. I review the key cases below.

[49] In Just for Laughs, several entities filed a notice of intention to make a proposal under the
BIA, and then the Court later authorized them and other entities to continue or commence
insolvency proceedings under the CCAA. As here, employees were terminated before proposed
transactions, however, the transactions involved both asset sales and RVO transactions. In the
RVO transactions, employee liabilities were transferred to the newly incorporated residual
company. Unlike this case, the question of whether the employees were entitled to WEPP
payments was brought before and decided by the Court before the Minister had made any decision
about entitlement and at the same time as the RVO transaction was approved.

[50] The Court held, at para 34, that the time to determine entitlement under WEPPA is “the
moment at which all an insolvent entity’s employees are terminated due to a bankruptcy or
restructuring”. In reaching this determination, the Court exercised its jurisdiction to determine
WEPP payment entitlement in the context of a CCAA or bankruptcy, as expressly provided for in
section 5(5) of WEPPA and section 3.2 of the Wage Earner Protection Program Regulations,
SOR/2008-222 (Regulations). Those sections provide, respectively:

       Prescribed criteria — other proceedings

       (5)     On application by any person, a court may, in proceedings under Division I
       of Part III of the Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act or under the Companies’ Creditors
       Arrangement Act, determine that the former employer meets the criteria prescribed
       by regulation.

               [...]

       Proceedings Under Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act or Companies’ Creditors
       Arrangement Act

       3.2    For the purposes of subsection 5(5) of the Act, a court may determine
       whether the former employer is the former employer all of whose employees in
       Canada have been terminated other than any retained to wind down its business
       operations.

[51]   The Court declared that the debtor companies were former employers under WEPPA.

[52] Just for Laughs is distinguishable from this case because the Receiver does not seek the
WEPPA Declaration under section 5(5) of WEPPA or section 3.2 of the Regulations. Notably,
WEPPA does not expressly provide the same express judicial jurisdiction to determine whether a
former employee meets the criteria under WEPPA or its Regulations in the context of a
receivership.

[53] As noted above, leave to appeal Just for Laughs was refused in Just for Laughs CA.
Vauclair JA was not satisfied the proposed appeal was prima facie meritorious, because Justice
                                                          12


Collier did not make a finding about eligibility for WEPP payments but was exercising the Court’s
broad discretion under WEPPA and section 3.2 of the Regulations.

[54] Just for Laughs has been followed in other CCAA and BIA proceedings. In Syndic
d’Intelgenx Corp, 2024 QCCS 3678, in the context of CCAA proceedings, employees were laid
off and the Court approved an RVO transaction which involved the transfer of employee liabilities
to a residual corporation. The Court followed Just for Laughs and, under section 5(5) of WEPPA,
declared the CCAA debtor company to be the former employer of the terminated employees. 8

[55] In Valeo Pharma (a CCAA case), the approved RVO transaction structure contemplated
that some employees (not yet terminated), could be retained by the purchaser, but if the purchaser
did not retain them, then the employees were transferred to the residual corporation and terminated.
Justice Collier made a declaration under section 5(5) of WEPPA and the Regulations that the
residual corporation was the former employer of those employees. In this case, leave to appeal was
granted in Valeo Pharma CA. As noted above, I understand this appeal remains under reserve.

[56] In Arrangement relating to Lion Electric Company/Compagnie électrique Lion, 2025
QCCS 2210, the determination of a case similar to Just for Laughs and Valeo Pharma was
adjourned pending the Court of Appeal’s decision in Valeo Pharma.

[57] In Synaptive, another CCAA case similar to Valeo Pharma, the CCAA debtor retained some
employees, but agreements with other employees whose employment had been terminated were
transferred to a residual corporation as part of an RVO transaction. Declaratory relief was sought
at the time the RVO transaction, but was adjourned to give the Attorney General of Canada time
to respond. The RVO transaction then closed, and the residual corporation went bankrupt. In those
circumstances, the Court exercised its jurisdiction under section 5(5) of WEPPA and the
Regulations, to declare that the residual corporation was a former employer under WEPPA, both
as a CCAA applicant and as a bankrupt. In the circumstances, the Court did not need to decide
whether the debtor company was also a former employer.

[58] The WEPPA statutory interpretation question has not yet been settled by an appellate court,
or in any decision of this Court. Further, the previous cases are distinguishable from the
circumstances before me. In the other cases, applications for the declarations sought were brought
at the time the RVO transaction was approved, and before the Minister decided or reviewed the
question of the terminated employees’ entitlement to WEPP payments. Further, the other decisions
were brought in the context of proceedings for which WEPPA expressly provides for judicial
jurisdiction to make declarations about whether an employer is a “former employer” under
WEPPA.

[59] In contrast, in this case the WEPPA Declarations are sought after the Revised RVO
transaction was approved and closed, and after the Minister had already made decisions about the
Former Employees’ entitlement to WEPP Payments. This different context materially changes the
question of whether this Court should consider making the WEPPA Declarations as requested.

forward was at the time the Revised RVO was approved.

[66] Fourth, WEPPA provides a statutory scheme governing the Minister’s review of
entitlement to WEPP payments, and the process for challenging the Minister’s decision. It provides
for the conditions of eligibility for WEPP payments (sections 5, 6); the Minister’s determination
whether an applicant is eligible to receiver a payment (section 9); the review of the Minister’s
determination of eligibility (sections 11, 12); and an appeal to the CIRB on questions of law or
jurisdiction (sections 13.1 to 20): WEPPA; Just for Laughs at para 23. Sections 19 and 20 of

Supreme Court of Canada decision in Democracy Watch v Canada (Attorney General), 2026 SCC
28).

[67] The Minister’s consideration of the legal effect of the Revised RVO, the Minister’s

[68] If the cases relied on by the Receiver are correct, then the Minister may very well have

reverse” the Minister’s review and rescission of the Former Employees’ eligibility for the WEPP
Payments as contemplated in para 46 of the Decision. I make no decisions about any of those
matters. At the late stage of these receivership proceedings, those matters are better resolved within
the existing WEPPA, CIRB appeal, and related Federal Court frameworks, not by this Court.

[69] In my view, WEPPA provided an alternative adequate statutory mechanism available to the
Former Employees post Revised RVO approval. Any residual unfairness following that process,
or otherwise arising out of WEPPA, is a matter for Parliament.

[70] Finally, the Receiver has not established that there is a practical utility or appropriate
purpose in this Court considering or making the WEPPA Declarations. The Minister has decided
the Former Employees are not entitled to the WEPP Payments. Asserting that the Minister’s
approach may offend the general sense of fairness of the Receiver or insolvency practitioners is,
without more, insufficient for this Court to intervene at this stage.

[71] The Former Employees did not participate in the applications before me. They provided no
evidence about what has occurred with respect to their WEPP Payments, the status regarding any
CIRB appeal or review process under WEPPA, or their intentions in that regard.

[72] With no other practical purpose to the declarations identified by the Receiver, one can only
speculate that the de facto purpose of the WEPPA Declarations may be to influence or be the
foundation for the Former Employees’ existing or future CIRB appeals, or perhaps a request for
the Minister to conduct another review of the Former Employees’ eligibility under WEPPA. I am
not satisfied that this justifies this Court considering or making the WEPPA Declarations at this
stage, base on the legal framework and considerations noted above.

[73] For the above reasons, I decline to exercise my discretion to consider or grant the WEPPA
Declarations.

                   2.       Legal Framework for Late Claims in Insolvency

[98] The objective of a claims procedure order is to ensure all legitimate creditors come forward
on a timely basis: Enron Canada Corp v National Oil-Well Canada Ltd, 2000 ABCA 285 [Blue
Range] at para 10, leave to appeal refused [2000] SCCA No 648; Re: SemCanada Crude
Company (Celtic Exploration Ltd #2), 2012 ABQB 489 [SemCanada] at para 62, leave to appeal
refused 2012 ABCA 313; UBG Builders Inc (Re), 2016 ABQB 472 at para 46; BA Energy Inc
(Re), 2010 ABQB 507 at para 41; Urbancorp Cumberland 2 GP Inc et al, 2023 ONSC 6747 at
para 106.

[99] Accordingly, and given the important role of the orderly discharge of a receiver’s duties
under a receivership, the acceptance of a late claim should not be an automatic outcome: Pacer
Construction Holdings Corporation v Pacer Promec Energy Corporation, 2018 ABCA 113 at
para 147, citing SemCanada at paras 53 and 62.

[100] Whether to extend the time to submit a proof of claim is a discretionary decision: West Bay
SonShip Yachts Ltd (Re), 2009 BCCA 31 [West Bay CA] at para 43. The onus is on the claimant:
Royal Bank of Canada v Cow Harbour Construction Ltd, 2011 ABQB 223 [Cow Harbour] at
para 27. The test engages equitable considerations having regard to the specific circumstances of
each case: Cow Harbour at para 27; BA Energy at para 34; UBG Builders at para 53. The applicant


10
     Filed September 29, 2026.
                                                    18


must provide “some explanation for the late filing”: Pacer at para 147; SemCanada at paras 62,
73; Urbancorp at para 104.

[101] The parties agree with the “appropriate criteria” courts apply to late claimants, from Blue
Range (a CCAA case) at para 26. This test has also been applied in receivership context: Pacer.

[102] The applicable criteria to apply is as follows:

       1.      Was the delay caused by inadvertence and if so, did the claimant act in good
               faith?

       2.      What is the effect of permitting the claim in terms of the existence and
               impact of any relevant prejudice caused by the delay?

       3.      If relevant prejudice is found can it be alleviated by attaching appropriate
               conditions to an order permitting late filing?

       4.      If relevant prejudice is found which cannot be alleviated, are there any other
               considerations which may nonetheless warrant an order permitting late
               filing?

[103] In this context, “inadvertent” includes “carelessness, negligence, accident, and is
unintentional”: Blue Range at para 27; Cow Harbour at para 26; UBG Builders at para 26; BA
Energy at para 35. This can include inadvertence of counsel: Cow Harbour at paras 32-33.
However, a deliberate choice, particularly when represented by counsel, is not inadvertence:
SemCanada at paras 52, 73; Arrangement relatif à 9323-7055 Québec inc (Aquadis
International Inc), 2019 QCCS 1396 [Aquadis] at para 13. Poor strategic choices will rarely be
grounds to file a proof of claim past the cut-off date: Montreal Maine & Atlantic Canada Co
(Plan of arrangement), 2015 QCCS 5896 at para 34, citing SemCanada.

[104] If there was nothing to be gained by the claimant’s delay, it may support a finding of
inadvertence: Cow Harbour at para 29.

[105] With respect to good faith, a simple assertion of good faith is not enough: Cow Harbour
at para 32. Submitting a claim as soon as the claimant becomes aware of the situation will support
a good faith finding: Pacer at para 157; Cow Harbour at para 32. On the other hand, attempting
to gain an advantage by late filing, improper manipulation of the process, otherwise lying in the
weeds, or delaying the application once the situation is known, will not: Blue Range at paras 18,
39; Cow Harbour at para 32; SemCanada at para 58; UBG Builders at paras 26; BA Energy at
para 42.

[106] With respect to “relevant prejudice” criteria, this can include prejudice to other creditors,
the debtor, other interested parties, or the court officer’s administration of the court-supervised
process: Pacer at para 150; BA Energy at para 49.

[107] Non-exhaustive considerations relevant to prejudice include:
                                                     19


       (a)     whether the late claimant’s claim was previously known: Blue Range at para 39;
               Cow Harbour at para 39;

       (b)     the length of the delay: Pacer at para 150; SemCanada at para 48; Urbancorp at
               para 104;

       (c)     the timing of the application within the court-supervised process: Blue Range at
               para 36; Cow Harbour at para 39; SemCanada at para 66;

       (d)     whether granting permission will cause further delay or expenditure of resources.
               This can include a delay in the finalization of the claims process: Pacer at para 150,
               citing West Bay SonShip Yachts Ltd, Re, 2007 BCSC 1553 at para 35 [West Bay
               SC], aff’d West Bay CA;

       (e)     whether a late claim causes another stakeholder to lose a realistic opportunity to do
               something it might otherwise have done: Blue Range at para 40; SemCanada at
               para 65; Target Canada Co (Re), 2017 ONSC 327 [Target Canada 2017] at paras
               27, 32. This may relate to decisions made, agreements reached or positions taken
               by stakeholders “on the basis of claims that had been filed as of the Claims Bar
               Date”: Pacer at para 168 (cited para 218); SemCanada at paras 64-65; Cow
               Harbour at para 39; BA Energy at paras 47, 51; Aquadis at para 22; Urbancorp at
               para 105, citing Timminco Limited (Re), 2014 ONSC 3393 at paras 41-44; Target
               Canada 2017 at para 41; West Bay SC at para 35; and

       (f)     the materiality of the late claim: Blue Range at para 37; Cow Harbour at para 40;
               SemCanada at para 67; BA Energy at para 46; West Bay SC at para 35. While the
               fact that other creditors will receive less money if late claims or amended claims
               are allowed is not, without more, relevant prejudice (Blue Range at paras 37, 40;
               Cow Harbour at para 41), the magnitude of the dilution of their recovery can be
               relevant to prejudice: Aquadis at para 22; Target Canada 2017 at para 31.

[108] With respect to the “other considerations” criterion, the Court may balance the equities,
including relevant prejudice suffered by the late claimant if the late claim is not allowed, versus
the prejudice if it is: SemCanada at para 73; BA Energy at para 58. Also, in this context, the
sufficiency or reasonableness of the reason for and the length of the delay are relevant
considerations: SemCanada at para 73; UBG Builders at para 65.

               3.      Inadvertence and Good Faith

[109] CNRL argues that its late submission of its proof of claim was inadvertent and in good
faith because it was focused on enforcing the Consent Order and Judgment against 269 Corp and
so was not actively monitoring developments in the receivership proceeding. It argues that it was
not focused on, or aware of the need to, dual-track its recovery efforts or maintain a watching brief
in the receivership. It notes that cure costs are generally a purchaser obligation, it was actively
enforcing against 269 Corp as the purchaser, and there was no reasonably foreseeable need to
monitor the receivership for claims-related matters. As noted, the CNRL employee’s evidence was
that she was not aware of the claims process for cure costs. CNRL also argues there was no
                                                     20


advantage to CNRL in late filing. Finally, CNRL points out that it was a known claimant and
CNRL should have been provided a claims package, but was not.

[110] The Receiver argues that CNRL’s failure to submit a timely claim was not inadvertent and
unintentional, but was the result of a deliberate strategic decision by which CNRL elected to pursue
an alternative recovery strategy against 269 Corp, which ostensibly provided it more certainty as
to timing and quantum of recovery. The Receiver relies on SemCanada and UBG Builders.

[111] In my view, this case highlights the important distinction between unintentional
carelessness and deliberate choices. As noted in Aquadis at para 14 (footnotes omitted):

       [14] The distinction between a party’s inadvertence, and its deliberate decision
       not to file a timely proof of claim, is relevant to the Court’s appreciation of whether
       the allowance of a late claim will adversely impact upon the “integrity and respect
       for the court process and its orders”. Clearly, it is more difficult for a claimant who
       has deliberately chosen not to file a timely proof of claim to demonstrate that it
       should be relieved from its default and treated equally with other parties who have
       respected the court process.

[112] The distinction between can sometimes be difficult to make. Justice Yamauchi commented
on this in Cow Harbour at para 26:

       The description of “inadvertent” in Blue Range, says that the action is
       “unintentional.” It is difficult to imagine a situation where carelessness, negligence
       or accident could be “intentional” and still be inadvertent, unless the court in that
       case was envisaging a situation where a claimant is wilfully blind or
       procrastinating.

[113] In my view, where a claimant is aware of its future right to make a claim, but then makes
a deliberate economic choice to stop monitoring, to ignore or be wilfully blind to, the ongoing
procedures in order to save money and pursue an alternative recovery strategy, the failure to submit
a timely claim cannot be said to be unintentional or inadvertent. In those circumstances, missing
the deadline to submit a claim is caused by a deliberate choice or strategy.

[114] The Blue Range criteria were never meant to save late claimants from poor or careless
decisions or strategies. Finding otherwise would significantly undermine the purpose of claims
procedures “to ensure all legitimate creditors come forward on a timely basis”.

[115] In this case, I find that CNRL was aware of its right to recover the CNRL Cure Costs from
the Share Transaction purchase proceeds. CNRL’s counsel was served with the Receiver’s
November 5, 2025 application for the approval of the Share Transaction, which included paragraph
10.1 of the proposed form of order (set out above in its entirety). The Receiver’s Second Report
also expressly flagged the Receiver’s intention to come back for approval of a claims process that
“will include potential Other Cure Costs claimants who may be owed amounts from the Purchase
                                                      21


Price Funds”. 11 CNRL attended the November 14, 2025 application and supported the application
because of the changed treatment for cure costs.

[116] In my view, in these circumstances CNRL cannot reasonably argue it was not aware of its
right to pursue a claim for the CNRL Cure Costs in a future claims process. The CNRL employee’s
evidence that she was unaware of the specific approved claims process and the Claims Procedure
Order until September 8, 2026, must be viewed in that context. In my view, her lack of knowledge
of the status of the receivership, the Claims Procedure Order, and the Claims Bar Date, was the
direct result, and the obvious and foreseeable outcome, of CNRL’s deliberate economic decision
not to monitor the receivership while it pursued recovery against 269 Corp. CNRL must be taken
to have known of the risk it would miss its opportunity to share in the proceeds of the Share
Transaction.

[117] In these circumstances, it cannot be said that CNRL’s delay was caused by inadvertence or
was unintentional. It is only after CNRL’s decision to focus on recovering from the purchaser did
not bear fruit, that it re-engaged with the receivership process, reviewed the Receiver’s application,
and filed a late claim. CNRL’s approach is akin to one of wilful blindness or procrastination, as
contemplated in Cow Harbour.

[118] I have considered the fact that the Receiver failed to provide CNRL a claims package. I
agree that CNRL was a “Known Claimant” (that is, one with a potential cure cost claim) and it
should have been sent the claims package.

[119] However, CNRL was served with the application to approve the Claims Procedure Order,
and the Receiver’s Third Report provided in support of that application. Those materials included
the proposed Claims Procedure Order (which in turn appended the claims package). These
materials went unnoticed by CNRL because it decided not to monitor the proceedings. Given
CNRL’s uncontradicted evidence that neither it nor its counsel were monitoring the receivership
at this time, had the claims package been sent it is likely it too would have gone unnoticed. In those
circumstances, the Receiver’s failure to provide the claims package does not change my decision
because it did not cause or contribute to CNRL’s delay.

[120] CNRL does not meet the first criterion.

                    4.      Prejudice

[121] CNRL argues that there is no prejudice to allowing its late claim. It points to the
jurisprudence that a diluted monetary recovery of other creditors is insufficient to establish relevant
prejudice. CNRL notes that the AER and the Municipalities’ priority is to ResidualCo’s funds, not
directly from the Share Transaction proceeds, and that it is only excess Share Transaction proceeds
(after payment of valid cure costs) that are paid to claimants of ResidualCo. Thus, CNRL argues
that there is nothing that AER or the Municipalities could do to overcome CNRL’s priority and,
therefore, they did not lose any realistic opportunity to do something different.

[122] In response, the Receiver argues that the final Settlement between the AER and the
Municipalities was reached without the knowledge of the CNRL Claim, that CNRL’s Cure Cost

                                                      22


Claim is material, and that it is highly possible that the reduction of recovery caused by CNRL’s
Cure Cost Claim would have led to a different result. In oral submissions, the AER and
Municipalities supported this position and argued that allowing CNRL’s late claim would cause
them prejudice. Counsel for the Municipalities advised that they proceeded on the understanding
that the Proposed Distribution would be uncontested.

[123] I find that there is relevant prejudice in these circumstances. As noted in SemCanada, at
para 64, it can reasonably be inferred after a claims bar date elapses that stakeholders make
decisions on the basis of the claims that had been filed as of the claims bar date. CNRL is a
sophisticated party that had been actively participating in the receivership proceedings, had
supported the Revised RVO, and had then publicly focused its recovery efforts by dealing directly
with 269 Corp. While there is no evidence that CNRL ever expressly advised the Receiver it was
foregoing its right to pursue cure costs in the receivership process, there is also no evidence that it
advised the Receiver or anyone else that it was preserving or intended to pursue those rights. It can
be reasonably inferred that the Receiver and other creditors proceeded on the basis that CNRL was
satisfied with its efforts against 269 Corp and did not intend to participate in the receivership claims
process.

[124] It can also reasonably be inferred that the AER and the Municipalities agreed to the
Settlement based on their understanding of the submitted claims, which did not include the CNRL
Cure Costs.

[125] The CNRL Cure Costs claim is material to the AER and Municipalities. If accepted, it
would reduce the surplus Share Purchase proceeds available to ResidualCo creditors by about
$88,000, from about $164,000 to about $76,000, which represents approximately a 24% reduction
in the amount available to the AER/Municipalities.

[126] The specific terms of the Settlement, other than the agreed sharing percentages, are not

CNRL Cure Costs claim, or whether the AER and the Municipalities would otherwise be entitled

[127] If the Settlement stands, then the AER and Municipalities will have lost their ability to
negotiate a settlement in light of the lower pool of funds available to them. I agree with the
Receiver that the lower amount available may very well have materially changed the negotiation
process or result as between these competing claimants. As per Aquadis at para 22, the decision
of the AER and the Municipalities to agree to the Settlement “would have been taken under a
misconception regarding its value to them, a prejudice that cannot now be alleviated”.

[128] If the Settlement does not stand, it would unwind an agreement and undermine the integrity
of the receivership process. There would be thrown away costs and a need for either new
negotiations or a litigation process to resolve the priority dispute between the AER and the
Municipalities. Both of these would delay the finalization of the receivership, increase costs of the
AER, the Municipalities and the Receiver, and create uncertainty in the receivership. The
receivership is otherwise ready to be finalized.

[129] In my view, whether the Settlement stands or falls, both scenarios create relevant prejudice.
On the record before me, CNRL has not satisfied me that the prejudice can be alleviated by
appropriate conditions.

               5.      Other Considerations

[130] As noted above, in deciding whether there are other considerations that warrant allowing
the late claim, relevant factors include the equities, the relative prejudice, and the reason for the
delay.

[131] In my view, the validity or merit of a late claim may also be a relevant “other
consideration”. The Receiver argues that the effect of the Side Agreement, the Consent Order and
the Judgment demonstrate an election by CNRL to pursue 269 Corp, or a “commercially
transferred liability”. Effectively, the Receiver argues that this acted as a release, and CNRL should
not be able to pursue both 269 Corp and ResidualCo for the CNRL Cure Costs, as that would give
it multiple avenues of recovery which would put it in a better position than other creditors. CNRL
disputes this position.

[132] The Receiver did not provide any legal authority for its position. In those circumstances,
and based on the terms of the Side Agreement and the Consent Order, I am not prepared to find
that CNRL released or made a binding election to forego its recovery of CNRL Cure Costs in the
receivership. I proceed on the basis that the CNRL Claim is valid.

[133] Notwithstanding that finding, I am not satisfied that there are other considerations that
support granting CNRL permission to allow its late claim. CNRL continues to have recourse as
against 269 Corp under the Judgment. Its decision to abandon monitoring the receivership has
turned out to have been a poor decision. That is not a reasonable excuse or explanation for its
delay. To preserve the integrity of the receivership process and the purpose of claims process
orders, I find it more appropriate that CNRL bears the consequences of its decisions and associated
delay. In my view, the prejudice to CNRL does not outweigh the prejudice to the Receiver, other
creditors, and the integrity of the receivership process.

               6.      Conclusion

[134] The late CNRL Claim is not allowed.

                 2.       Approval of Fees and Disbursements of the Receiver and its Legal
                          Counsel

[140] The onus is on a court officer to satisfy the court that the remuneration claimed is justified
and reflects fair compensation for the services provided: Patel v Chief Medical Supplies Ltd, 2019
ABQB 690 at para 32(a); Winalta Inc (Re), 2011 ABQB 399 at para 32; Piikani Nation v Piikani
Energy Corporation, 2011 ABQB 450 at para 13, citing Re Omni Data Supply Ltd, 2002 BCSC
1631 at paras 24-25 (in turn citing Re Hess (1977), 23 CBR (NS) 215). Insolvency professionals
are expected to work efficiently and reasonably, and to charge fair amounts for the services
provided.

[141] The question is whether the fees are fair and reasonable in all the circumstances: Coast
Automotive Group Inc (Re), 2026 ABKB 490 at para 72; Bank of Nova Scotia v Diemer, 2014
ONCA 851 [Diemer] at paras 32-33, 45; Winalta at para 30; Hendley (Re), 2016 ABQB 721 at
para 20. This is consistent with the Receivership Order, which follows the Alberta template
receivership order in providing that “the Receiver and counsel to the Receiver shall be paid their
reasonable fees and disbursements”.

[142] Several cases provide non-exhaustive relevant factors that serve as a useful guide in
determining whether the fees of a court-officer and its legal counsel are fair and reasonable. A
combined non-exhaustive list from some of these cases includes:

        (a)      the nature, extent and value of the assets;

        (b)      the complications and difficulties encountered;

        (c)      the degree of assistance provided by the parties or others;

        (d)      time spent;

       (f)     diligence and thoroughness;

[143] As is common, in this case there was no objection raised to the approval of the accounts of
the Receiver and its counsel. In my view, this factor supports an inference of fairness and
reasonableness and weighs in favour of approval.

[144] The Receiver provided summaries of accounts in the Receiver’s Fourth Report. It did not
follow the practice, contemplated in Confectionately Yours, of providing an affidavit proving the
accounts. Instead, the Receiver offered to provide the Court the accounts upon request if necessary.

[145] In my view, where an impacted or potentially impacted party requests it, or objects to the
fees of a court officer and its counsel, the court-officer is obligated to prove the fees and
disbursements by way of affidavits including the detailed accounts: Confectionately Yours at paras
38-41; Patel at para 32(c).

[146] In those circumstances, the court officer may redact information, for example to protect
privilege: Laurentian University of Sudbury, 2022 ONSC 5850 at para 5. However, if required
redactions render the accounts misleading, incomprehensible, or insufficient to support the fee
claim, or otherwise create unfairness, the better practice in a disputed case is to also provide an
unredacted copy of the affidavit with the accounts to the Court and, if necessary, seek the sealing
of that affidavit; otherwise the court-officer may not be able to discharge its onus: see, for example:
Hanfeng Evergreen Inc, (Re), 2017 ONSC 7161 at paras 29-30; MBH v CKI, 2023 ABKB 284
at para 42-43; Sunstrum Estate (Re), 2026 ABKB 491 at para 135; Patel v Chief Medical Supplies
Ltd, 2021 ABQB 355 at para 130.

[147] However, where the persons that are de facto at risk for or directly impacted (or potentially
impacted) by the approval of the fees, are sophisticated insolvency participants, or are otherwise
represented by counsel, and those persons do not request further information or object to the fees

and disbursements, as a matter of practicality and efficiency the court has discretion to accept more
streamlined evidence. For example, in those circumstances a summary of accounts in a court
officer’s report that also describes the work performed may suffice. Account summaries in court
officer reports should generally include the invoice amounts charged, disbursements, together with
the timekeeper identities, hourly rates and hours worked for the timeframe covered.

[148] Having said that, courts retain the discretion to require further information, including the
detailed accounts proven by way of affidavit, where it is necessary and appropriate to discharge
their supervisory role and ensure that the amount charged is fair and reasonable. Court officers
seeking to avoid disputes or surprise and associated delay might consider providing fee affidavits
regardless of whether there is an objection.

[149] In any event, anything provided to the Court for its consideration should be filed to provide
a meaningful record for appellate review, if necessary.

[150] I have reviewed the evidence of the fees and disbursements of the Receiver and its counsel,
and the activities of the Receiver during the relevant period as reflected in the Receiver’s Reports.
Noting no objection by any party, the quantum of the accounts, and the factors noted above, I find
they are fair and reasonable and they are approved.

               3.      Approval of Proposed Distribution

[151] The Court has well-established authority to approve distributions: Realtrium Holdings
Pickering Inc v Simpson, 2025 ONSC 5605 at para 31.

[152] No party objected to the Proposed Distribution. It is reasonable and approved. This is
subject to my comments noted above with respect to the Superpriority Amount. My approval of
the Proposed Distribution is not a decision about the appropriate handling of the Superpriority
Amount going forward. The Receiver, ESDC and the Former Employees should attempt to reach
resolution of that question, failing which any party can seek the Court’s determination, if
necessary. The form of order can be amended to reflect this paragraph.

               4.      Discharge

[153] The proposed discharge is not opposed and is reasonable in the circumstances. I am
satisfied that the steps necessary to resolve the Superpriority Amount can be addressed as an
administrative matter within the estimated future Receiver and counsel fees, as contemplated in
the proposed form of order.

[154] The Court declines to consider or grant the proposed WEPPA Declarations. CNRL’s

amendments as may be required to implement my decisions above. The Receiver may seek my
further directions if required to finalize the order.
