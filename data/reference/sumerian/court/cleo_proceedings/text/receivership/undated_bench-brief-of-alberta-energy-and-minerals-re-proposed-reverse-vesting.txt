            PART I:           Facts

            1.     Overview of the Application

            [1]    The proposed Reverse Vesting Order (the “CLEO RVO”) aims to vest pre-receivership
            Crown Liabilities (defined below) in “ResidualCo”, a shell corporation, while Mineral Leases
            (defined below) remain in the name of the debtor, CLEO Energy Ltd. (“CLEO Energy”). As
            structured, the CLEO RVO allows CLEO Energy to retain the benefits of the Mineral Leases
            while eschewing previously accumulated royalties owed to the public.

            [2]    Reverse vesting orders are intended to be used rarely and extraordinarily. There is nothing
            rare or extraordinary about the CLEO receivership. The proposed CLEO RVO is a blatant
            attempt to circumvent the Crown in right of Alberta’s (the “Alberta Crown”) ability to recover
            its cure costs and manage public resources.

            [3]    The Minister of Energy and Minerals (through the Alberta Department of Energy and
            Minerals (“Alberta Energy”)) opposes any relief sought in the CLEO RVO, or otherwise, that
            seeks to saddle the public with CLEO Energy’s liabilities while allowing CLEO Energy to retain
            Mineral Leases.

            2.      Statement of Facts

            [4]    The facts set out below are relevant for the purposes of this Application.

                  (a) CLEO Energy’s Crown Liabilities

            [5]    The Alberta Crown owns 81% of the province’s mineral rights. 1 Alberta Energy
            administers, manages, and regulates these resources on behalf of the citizens of the Province. 2

            [6]     The Alberta Crown offers its petroleum and natural gas (“Crown PNG”) mineral rights
            for bid by industry. Successful bidders obtain a Crown PNG Lease (a “Mineral Lease”), which
            grants the lessee the right to drill for, and recover, Crown PNG within the lease area. 3

            [7]    The Mineral Lease does not grant mineral ownership to the lessee; ownership remains with
            the Alberta Crown. The PNG minerals are publicly owned and the lessee must pay for each unit
            produced, whether oil or natural gas. This payment is a “royalty”. A royalty is not a tax, rather it
            is a payment to the public for the use of its minerals. 4

            [8]    Mineral Leases for Crown PNG minerals identify two lessee obligations to the Alberta
            Crown (“Crown Liabilities”): (1) an ongoing obligation to pay royalties to the Alberta Crown
            for all minerals produced; and (2) an ongoing obligation to pay for the right to win, work, and
            recover minerals on Alberta Crown land. 5

            [9]    Currently, CLEO Energy holds Mineral Leases and has Crown Liabilities totalling
            $240,000.00, of which $150,000.00 consists of pre-receivership Crown Liabilities. While the
            Receiver in this matter appears to have confirmed that the $90,000.00 in post-filing Crown
            Liabilities will be paid by either it or the Purchaser, there is no such guarantee for the pre-filing
            Crown Liabilities. 6

                  (b) The CLEO RVO

            [10] Under the CLEO RVO and the proposed Share Purchase Agreement (“CLEO SPA”), the
            pre-receivership Crown Liabilities are proposed to be vested to “ResidualCo.”, a shell
            corporation, while the operating entity, CLEO Energy, retains the assets (in this case, the Mineral
            Leases) and continues operations. The purchaser would acquire CLEO Energy’s shares, not its
            assets: 7

            PART III: Argument

            1.       Harte Gold factors

            [12] As Justice Penny noted in Harte Gold, the use of RVO structures “should continue to be
            regarded as an unusual or extraordinary measure; not an approach appropriate in any case merely
            because it may be more convenient or beneficial for the purchaser”. 8

            [13] In the decision, the Court articulated the factors to be considered in respect of a proposed
            reverse vesting transaction:

                 •    Why is the reverse vesting order necessary in this case?

                 •    Does the reverse vesting structure produce an economic result at least as favourable as
                      any other viable alternative?

                 •    Is any stakeholder worse off under the reverse vesting structure than they would have
                      been under any other viable alternative?

                 •    Does the consideration being paid for the debtor’s business reflect the importance and
                      value of assets being preserved under the reverse vesting structure? 9

                 (a) Alberta Energy is worse off under the CLEO RVO

            [14] Alberta Energy is detrimentally impacted under the proposed CLEO RVO.

            [15] Section 18(2) of the Mines and Minerals Act provides the Minister with discretion to refuse
            the transfer of a Crown mineral agreement. 10 This provision grants the Minister broad discretion
            to assess the suitability of any proposed transferee of a Crown agreement. The discretion is
            rooted in public policy considerations, including the financial and operational capacity of the
            transferee to fulfill obligations under the agreement, such as royalty payments.

            [16] In this case, under a traditional asset purchase arrangement, Alberta Energy would have the
            ability to require the payment of cure costs (i.e., the Crown Liabilities – the monetary defaults
            under the Mineral Leases) before agreeing to transfer the Mineral Leases to the Purchaser (the
            “Transfer Requirement”). 11

            [17] The CLEO RVO circumvents that discretion as the Mineral Leases would remain in
            CLEO’s name. In other words, there would be no transfer of assets.

            [18] The Transfer Requirement is public knowledge and is routinely enforced by Alberta
            Energy in insolvency asset transactions. 12 Both industry and receivers/trustees are aware of the
            Transfer Requirement. 13

            [19] The Alberta Crown has a vested interest in ensuring that mineral resources are developed
            responsibly and that royalty obligations are met.

            [20] The Purchaser’s refusal to address all cure costs raises serious questions regarding its
            intention, financial capacity, and operational capacity to fulfill its obligations under the Mineral
            Leases going forward because:

                 •   No information has been provided about the entity that is proposed to purchase CLEO
                     Energy’s shares; 14 and

                 •   The CLEO RVO is intentionally structured to allow the debtor to shed its Crown
                     Liabilities, circumvent the Transfer Requirement, and still keep the Mineral Leases.

            [21] These concerns are only heightened when considering that the information provided by the
            Receiver to date strongly suggests that ResidualCo. will have little ability to satisfy the Crown
            Liabilities, Transferring liabilities to a shell entity with no assets or operational capacity
            frustrates the Alberta Crown’s ability to enforce compliance, collect royalties, or hold entities

            PART IV:             Conclusion and Relief Sought

            [22] The Applicants have not established that a reverse vesting order is appropriate in the
            circumstances. Rather, this transaction appears to be advanced simply because it is “more
            convenient or beneficial for the purchaser”.

            [23] The Court should be wary of the proliferation of the use of reverse vesting orders to
            extinguish public debts without compensation. These types of transactions should be closely
            scrutinized to discourage the use of this type of structure to avoid paying royalties, dump the
            liability on a shell entity, while maintaining the ability to work public resources. 15

            [24] Those portions of the application of the CLEO RVO that deal with the pre-receivership
            Crown Liabilities, should be dismissed. In the alternative, if the Court does find the CLEO RVO
            and CLEO SPA to be appropriate, the Court should direct that all of the Crown Liabilities be
            cured.
