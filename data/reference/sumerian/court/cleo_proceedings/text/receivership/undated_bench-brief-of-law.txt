I.     INTRODUCTION

1.     This Bench Brief of Law is filed in support of the Application of Alvarez & Marsal Canada
       Inc. (“A&M”), in its capacity as Court-appointed receiver and manager (the “Receiver”) of
       the undertakings, properties and assets of CLEO Energy Corp. (“Cleo” or the “Company”)
       for Orders which provide the following relief, among other things:

       (a)    approving a share purchase agreement between the Receiver and 2698902
              Alberta Corp. (“269” or the “Purchaser”) dated October 6, 2025 (the “SPA”), for
              the sale by the Receiver and the purchase by the Purchaser of all of the issued

A.          The SPA and the Transaction Should be Approved, and the RVO Granted

            Principles Governing Receivership Sales

4.          Section 243(1)(c) of the Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act, RSC 1985, c B-3 (the “BIA”) and
            the Receivership Order at paragraph 3 authorize the Receiver to market and sell the
            Company’s property outside the ordinary course of business, subject to the approval of
            the Court.1

5.          The Court’s discretion to approve or decline a proposed transaction in a receivership is
            guided by the Soundair principles, being:

            (a)    whether the receiver has made a sufficient effort to obtain the best price and has
                   not acted improvidently;

            (b)    the interests of all parties;

            (c)    the efficacy and integrity of the process by which offers are obtained; and

            (d)    whether there has been unfairness in the working out of the process.2

6.          The Soundair principles should be applied flexibly, in a manner that gives deference to a
            receiver’s commercial expertise in a given factual circumstance, including such things as
            the prevailing economic environment, and the risks or rewards associated with an
            extended or additional sales process.3

                 the Purchaser has expressed that it is prepared to fulfill these obligations;10

        Principles Governing Reverse Vesting Transactions

10.     This Court has jurisdiction to approve RVO transactions pursuant to sections 183 and 243
        of the BIA.14 The question to be addressed by a court generally when determining whether
        to approve an RVO transaction is whether the relief sought is appropriate, and whether
        stakeholders are being treated fairly and reasonably in light of the surrounding
        circumstances.15

11.     While RVOs should not be the “norm”, courts have found RVOs to be necessary and
        appropriate in circumstances where the debtor operates in a heavily regulated industry,
        and the licensing and permitting which are necessary to the debtor’s business would be
        difficult to transfer or assign.16

12.        Here, the Company operates in the heavily regulated oil and gas industry. The licenses
           and permits necessary to operate the Company’s business would be difficult to transfer or
           assign, and would add time, risk and expense to the Transaction if they were required to
           be transferred to the Purchaser.

13.        Courts consider factors from Harte Gold to decide whether to approve an RVO
           transaction.17 These factors are as follows:

           (a)    whether the RVO is necessary in the circumstances;

           (b)    whether the RVO structure produces an economic result at least as favourable as
                  any other alternative;

           (c)    whether the general stakeholders are not worse off under the RVO structure than
                  they would have been under any other viable alternative; and

           (d)    whether the consideration being paid for the debtor’s business reflects the
                  importance and value of the intangible assets being preserved under the RVO
                  structure.18

14.        While Harte Gold proceeded under the Companies’ Creditors Arrangement Act, courts
           have applied its principles in the context of RVOs in receivership proceedings.19

15.        The Receiver has addressed each factor of the Harte Gold test in detail at paragraphs 47
           – 55 of the First Report. In brief, the Transaction and the RVO sought satisfy the Harte
           Gold test for the following reasons:

           (a)    The RVO structure is necessary in this case due to the AER licenses involved. If
                  an RVO structure is not used, the Purchaser would need to seek approval from the
                  AER for these licenses, which could be a lengthy and costly undertaking, and
                  which would add risk to the transaction.20 Further, the Purchaser has advised the
                  Receiver that it is only prepared to proceed with the Transaction if it is in the RVO
                  format.21

B.         The Releases Sought in the RVO Should be Granted

16.        Courts apply the factors set out in Lydian International Limited (Re) when determining
           whether to approve releases contemplated in RVOs.25 These factors are as follows:

           (a)    whether the parties to be released from claims were necessary and essential to
                  the restructuring of the debtor;

           (b)    whether the claims to be released were rationally connected to the purpose of the
                  plan and necessary for it;

           (c)    whether the plan could succeed without the releases;

           (d)    whether the parties being released were contributing to the plan; and

           (e)    whether the release benefitted the debtors and the creditors generally.26

24.     The test for whether a temporary sealing order should be granted is based on the following
        factors from Sherman Estate v Donovan, 2021 SCC 25:

        (a)      whether court openness poses a serious risk to an important public interest;

        (b)      whether the order sought is necessary to prevent this serious risk to the identified
                 interest because reasonable alternative measures will not prevent this risk; and

        (c)      as a matter of proportionality, the benefits of the order outweigh its deleterious
                 effects.31

25.     Since Sherman Estate, courts have continued to find that sealing orders are justified to
        protect the integrity of a receiver’s sales or marketing efforts and to avoid the misuse of
        information by bidders in a subsequent process to obtain an unfair advantage.32

26.     The Confidential Appendices contain a summary of the Non-Binding Bids submitted to the
        Receiver in the Remarketing Process and an unredacted copy of the SPA.33

27.     The Sherman Estate factors are met, and the Confidential Appendices should be
        temporarily sealed, for the following reasons:

        (a)      The information contained in the Confidential Appendices would harm any future
                 realization efforts for the Remaining Assets if the Transaction fails to close.34

        (b)      There are no reasonable alternative measures that could reduce the risk of the
                 above harm.

        (c)      The Receiver is not aware of any prejudice to any stakeholders given the sealing
                 order sought is temporary and limited in scope.35

        (d)      The salutary effects of temporarily sealing the Confidential Appendices outweigh
                 the deleterious effects.

D.         The Receiver’s Actions and Activities Should be Approved

28.        This Court has jurisdiction to review and approve the activities of a court-appointed
           receiver.36

29.        A receiver’s conduct is to be assessed objectively, and a Court should approve the
           activities set out in a receiver’s report if the activities are reasonable, prudent, and not
           arbitrary.37 Further, where a receiver has fulfilled the purpose of obtaining a high value for
           the debtor’s assets, the court should find that the receiver has acted properly and within
           its mandate.38

30.        The Receiver’s activities since the Receivership Date are set out in detail at paragraph 13
           of the First Report.39 Some of the Receiver’s activities during this time included (i) taking
           possession of the Property and performing a physical walk-through of the same; (ii)
           mailing BIA section 245 and 246 notices to the Company’s known creditors on June 11,
           2025; (iii) meeting with the Company’s employees to explain the powers and duties of a
           receiver; (iv) terminating all employees of the Company on June 3, 2025; (v) undertaking
           management of the Company’s daily operations; (vi) establishing and initiating the
           Remarketing Process; (vii) meeting with the AER to discuss the Remarketing Process and
           bids received; and (viii) negotiating and progressing the SPA, among other things.40

31.        The Receiver’s actions and activities are reasonable and prudent, and are consistent with
           its mandate. Further, the Receiver has obtained a high value for the assets subject to the
           SPA. The Receiver’s actions and activities should be approved together with the
           Receiver’s receipts and disbursements, and those of its counsel, as set out in the First
           Report.

E.      The Receiver and its Counsel’s Fees and Disbursements Should be Approved

32.     In Bank of Nova Scotia v Diemer,41 the Ontario Court of Appeal applied the non-exhaustive
        factors originally set out in Belyea v Federal Business Development Bank42 to be
        considered to determine whether a receiver’s fees are fair and reasonable:

        (a)      the nature and extent of the value of the assets handled;

        (b)      the complications and difficulties encountered;

        (c)      the degree of assistance provided by the company, its officers or employees;

        (d)      the time spent;

        (e)      the receiver’s knowledge, experience and skill;

        (f)      the diligence and thoroughness displayed by the receiver;

        (g)      the responsibilities assumed;

        (h)      the results of the receiver’s efforts; and

        (i)      the cost of comparable services.

33.     The Receiver’s fees, and those of its counsel, were incurred in connection with the
        Receiver’s activities described at paragraph 30 hereof. These activities required diligence,
        thoroughness, significant time expenditure, and for the Receiver to be heavily involved in
        the operations of the Company.

34.     The Receiver submits that its fees, and the fees of its counsel, are fair and reasonable
        given the substantive tasks required to be performed in connection with the Receivership
        Proceedings.43
